Executive Summary 
The purpose of this study is to develop a repeatable methodology and baseline metrics for 
tracking trends in heat pump project costs over time that will assist with quantifying market effects 
from the PAs’ support of residential heat pumps in Massachusetts. This includes centrally ducted 
air-source heat pumps (ASHP), ground -source heat pumps (GSHP), heat pump water heaters 
(HPWH), and mini-split heat pumps (MSHP). MSHPs are also variously known as multi-split heat 
pumps and ductless air-source heat pumps, although they also come in ducted configurations. 
In this study NMR analyzed a sample of 227 ASHP, 490 MSHP, 7 GSHP and 69 HPWH projects 
across Massachusetts supported by the electric and gas energy efficiency Program 
Administrators (PAs) that sponsor Mass Save ® (Figure 1 ). These heat pumps were installed 
primarily in existing homes between 2021 and 2022. NMR also analyzed the invoices of 94 Mass 
Save central air conditioner (CAC) projects , expecting that they would provide a useful 
comparison for ASHP since CAC are effectively heat pumps that only operate in cooling mode. 
We supplemented the Mass Save sample with 11 ASHP, 2,212 MSHP and 186 GSHP projects 
dating to between 2019 and 2021 from databases published by the Massachusetts Clean Energy 
Center (MassCEC). The results of our analysis (methods in Section 1.3) comprise the bulk of this 
report in Section 2 through Section 6, the highlights of which are summarized on the next page. 
Figure 1: Mass Save Program Invoice Samples by Equipment Type and Region 
The regions in Figure 1  are Core-Based Statistical Areas (CBSA). These areas  group 
economically coupled communities together and may cross state boundaries . For example, 
Rhode Island and Bristol County, MA comprise the Providence-Warwick CBSA (Section 1.3.4).

KEY FINDINGS 
➢ The fraction of project costs attributed to e quipment prices have increased for all 
HVAC categories: centrally ducted ASHP  (Section 4.4), ductless MSHP (Section 2.4), 
ducted/mixed MSHP (Section 3.4) and GSHP (Section 5.4). 
➢ In contrast , trends in overall project costs are mixed . Inflation-adjusted d uctless 
MSHP project costs have increased slightly (Section 2.2), while those for ducted/mixed 
MSHP have decreased (Section 3.2), but no statistically significant trend is apparent for  
either ASHP (Section 4.2) or GSHP (Section 5.2). 
➢ ASHP costs decrease when they are part of a larger project that includes installation 
of other equipment such as a furnace (Section 4.2.1). 
➢ GSHP costs are lower for new construction than they are for existing homes , 
although it is primarily the equipment and installation costs that exhibit the most substantial 
difference, not the ground-loop costs (Section 5.7). 
➢ Wiring does not represent a substantial portion of project  costs, and service 
upgrades are uncommon in the sample of program projects examined in this study. It is 
possible that most homes that would require a service upgrade choose to forgo the 
installation of heat pumps , but another possibility is that e xisting capacity is sufficient to 
meet the load of the new equipment  (e.g., the replacement of resistance heating or a 
central air-conditioner with a heat pump). 
➢ There is notable homogeneity among regional wages for heat pump-related trades, 
although there are some trends across equipment types suggesting wages in Hampden, 
Hampshire, and Franklin counties were lower than the rest of the state during the period 
the study covers, while Dukes and Nantucket counties were higher (Appendix A.7). 
CONCLUSIONS 
Our analysis has found evidence that the distribution of inflation-adjusted costs within a project 
shifted toward higher equipment costs over the study period, but overall project costs do not show 
the same trend. This suggests that from 2019 to 2022–2023, equipment costs increased faster 
than inflation while the rate was slower for  other project costs. In general, one would expect 
equipment costs to decrease as the scale of adoption increases and manufacturing techniques 
improve.1 However, numerous factors in recent years, such as shipping disruptions in the Suez 
and Panama canals (2021 and 2023–2024 respectively) and other supply chain constraints have 
served to drive up prices. It is also possible that changes in equipment mark -up practices by 
contractors have contributed to this trend. Given these confounding factors and inconsistencies 
across related equipment categories in our sample, we have little to no evidence at present that 
supports (or refutes) the hypothesis that program incentives drive up heat pump project costs. 
 
1 An alternative trend followed by some technologies is  for costs to remain about the same  but for specifications to

CONSIDERATIONS 
The team can periodically repeat the  methods and analyses laid out in this report to 
provide updated information on the residential heat pump markets and  inform 
understanding of the impacts of Mass Save ’s residential heat pump offerings. Future 
studies extending the time series could inform PA program planning as well as  feed into other 
research studies  that need cost information,  including the annual heat pump market effects 
studies and the incremental cost studies performed under Residential contract. Since heat pump 
costs are among the market effects indicators meant to be reported with the annual heat pump 
market effects studies, it may be useful to conduct the heat pump invoice analysis annually, timed 
to incorporate  indicator-specific results into the market effects  study report . Because rebate 
applications are frequently submitted several months after a project is installed and may 
experience further delays in processing, a single program year’s data may include projects 
spanning multiple years. Invoices obtained for future studie s are likely to yield additional 
information about previous years’ heat pump costs, so future studies could also be used to 
augment and verify or refine past year’ results. 
While web-scraped costs are an imperfect touchstone for participant costs due to differences in 
channel sourcing, sampling frequency, et cetera, they can nevertheless serve as a useful indicator 
of broader market conditions due to their provenance as non -program data. To ensure that the 
scraped costs are available when needed, they could be collected on a periodic basis—perhaps 
once a year—and then analyzed when they are needed for  other evaluation activities, such as 
informing incremental cost studies or updating certain parameters in the enhanced dataset. 
It should also be noted that the preliminary web-scraping results from this study were provided to 
Guidehouse for use in their most recent incremental cost study ( MA22R51-B-HPMS). The 
enhanced dataset pairs these costs with individual sampled program projects and their itemized 
invoice costs, which could be used to further review or revise the incremental costs.

Revised program intake forms and tracking systems would simplify cost data review and 
analysis. The rebate forms in this study d id not include fields such as equipment disposition 
(replacement versus supplementary supply), or MSHP equipment configuration (ducted, ductless 
or mixed). While these parameters would be useful for classifying participants, they would also 
compete with a need to reduce bureaucratic friction for participants. 
Traditionally, each rebate form has been assigned a unique ID by the program implementor, but 
frequently many pieces of equipment are installed at the same time and there does not appear to 
be an identifier tying these separate rebates  together. The lack of such a unifying identifier 
significantly complicates efforts to compare program tracking data against invoice samples 
received in response to a data request. 
NMR also noted another need to improve the project cost field of the implementor’s tracking 
system in the April 2024 Transcribed Data and Invoice Comparison memo produced as part of 
this study. We found the project cost field unreliable because it sometimes included data entry 
mistakes, the cost of more than one measure, or non-program work. 
Consider improving future rounds of heat pump cost-tracking by expanding the data 
sources used and devising a database -driven collection form to increase process 
efficiency. NMR made use of the PAs’ Qualified Product List (QPL) in this study, but in reviewing 
older invoices and web-scraped products we encountered many models that were not on the 
QPL. Purchasing access to NEEP’s Cold-Climate ASHP Specification Product List or the AHRI 
Certification Directory would improve the efficiency of verifying the specifications of scraped and 
incentivized products. We also developed a spreadsheet data collection form for invoice data at 
the beginning of the study, but over time learned that such an approach was too cumbersome 
given the variations in invoicing practices among contractors.  Devising a database -driven 
collection form to allow for free entry of individual invoice items  would streamline reviewing 
invoices, ultimately lowering costs over time. 
Given our findings regarding seasonal and regional variations in costs, future studies 
might incorporate these factors into their sampling plan.  However, doing so is likely to 
increase project costs and implementer response burden given the increased sample sizes 
required, particularly if future data request responses are of a similar unstratified nature to those 
received in this study. For both data requests NMR made to the implementors in this study, we 
requested specific numbers of invoices for different types of equipment  (e.g., 210 HPWH) but 
instead received a large, unsorted collection of invoices for both heat pump and non -heat pump 
projects. Sorting through this stack required considerable unforeseen effort, which would increase 
with larger samples or additional quota variables such as installation season. To solve this, NMR 
could randomly sample projects from the program tracking data and request rebate 
documentation for the se projects by rebate ID , if the implementor can easily handle such 
requests. Alas, this may bias the sample since we observed in this study that approximately one-
third of the invoices we processed were not within the heat pump project tracking data we had 
available. It is not clear why this is the case, but there are numerous plausible scenarios that could 
contribute to this  outcome. For example,  the invoices were  attributed to and tracked under a 
different program year, the applicant sought a rebate of some of the listed  equipment but not 
others, or a duplicate application may have been received and assigned a different number.

Future studies should consider the influence various program and non-program factors 
may have on heat pump project costs  and cost trends . Changes to program incentives or 
eligibility requirements are obvious potential influences, but other factors—such as the “Silver 
Tsunami’s” impact on th e trades work force as the baby -boomer generation retires —are more 
easily overlooked. Another influence is the phase-out of many HFC refrigerants. Although, given 
their age, we expect the projects in this study to be minimally affected by the EPA’s regulation of 
HFC refrigerants under the Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol, future studies should 
recognize the potential disruptions to the market as manufacturers redesign their products to use 
refrigerants with a lower global warming potential  (GWP). Specifically, i mports of high-GWP 
residential HVAC equipment will be banned after January 1, 2025 , and any covered stock must 
be installed before January 1, 2026.2,3 
A potential impact of changes to program incentives is for contractors to adjust the m ark-up 
invoiced on equipment to capture more value, so examining changes in mark-up over time could 
be instructive . Unfortunately, mark -up is a particularly opaque  aspect of invoicing practice. 
However, subsequent studies could seek to probe trends in this practice through various 
techniques. One possible, if imperfect, approach would be to  examine the average ratio of 
itemized equipment cost to web-scraped cost over time to focus on any change of inferred mark-
up rather than the calculated rate itself. Based on some preliminary analysis of the data from this 
study which revealed no obvious longitudinal trends for either ductless or ducted MSHP, 
additional sample may be required to help ensure an adequate number of invoices with itemized 
equipment costs are available to allow for robust statistical analysis. 
Another, similar, factor which may total project costs is the possibility of selective price increases 
by distributors and retailers compared to other regions. Such behavior could be motivated by a 
desire to capture a larger proportion of program incentives. We could explore this in the next study 
by collecting prices from vendors with multiple locations  to compare the listed prices for 
Massachusetts and a similar region with a less generous or non-existent heat pump offering.

Section 1 Introduction 
This Residential Heat Pump Cost Tracking (MA23X14-B-RHPINV) study seeks to document the 
costs associated with program -incentivized residential heat pump installations and develop 
metrics for tracking heat pump cost trends over time.  It was prepared  for the Massachusetts 
Program Administrators (PAs) and the Energy Efficiency Advisory Council (EEAC) consultants by 
the Massachusetts Cross-Cutting Research Area evaluation team, led by NMR Group. 
1.1 GOALS 
The overarching purpose of this study is to develop a repeatable methodology and baseline 
metrics for tracking trends in heat pump project costs over time that will assist with quantifying 
market effects from the PAs’ support of residential heat pumps in M assachusetts. This includes 
centrally ducted air-source heat pumps (ASHP), ground-source heat pumps (GSHP), heat pump 
water heaters (HPWH), and mini-split heat pumps (MSHP). MSHP are also variously known as 
multi-split heat pumps and ductless air-source heat pumps, although they also come in ducted 
configurations. Per the original work plan, this study aims to: 
1. Establish and document a methodology for long-term tracking of the labor and materials 
costs of heat pump projects using labor and materials/equipment cost data from the 
invoices of participating projects and equipment costs from web -scraping sites of 
Massachusetts distributors and online wholesalers. 
2. Extract as much information as possible from project invoices about the work done at 
each sampled installation and analyze the impact of various job attributes (e.g., panel 
upgrades, use of integrated controls, addition or changes to ductwork, removal of 
previous equipment, listed rebates) on overall invoiced project costs, exclusive of 
program incentives. 
3. Identify the key variables impacting project costs, recommend normalized cost metrics 
using these variables, and establish baseline values for these metrics for comparison 
against future measurements. 
1.2 DATA SOURCES 
This study employs two sources of project cost data: 1) Mass Save program rebate applications 
for heat pumps and heat pump water heaters, and 2) public databases of heat pump project costs 
published by the Massachusetts Clean Energy Center (MassCEC). 
1.2.1 Project Cost Data 
Mass Save program rebate applications. The Mass Save rebate data comprised information 
from PA program tracking data for the Residential Coordinated Delivery and Residential Retail 
initiatives supplemented with project invoices that participating installers or customers submitted 
as part of their rebate applications.  For the program trackin g data, NMR leveraged a 
comprehensive set of 2021 and 2022 residential heat pump and CAC  tracking data already on 
hand from the Heat Pump Market Effects Indicators Baseline study (MA22X06-B-HPMEIB).

served by natural gas, 5  while the earlier program was open to all program administrator 
customers.

1.3 METHODOLOGY 
This section describes the techniques used to prepare the data in this study. 
1.3.1 Program Data Preparation, Sampling & Invoice Transcription 
Once NMR received a response to a data request, we removed duplicate files from program 
implementor’s records  and converted the myriad image formats  among them to PDF . We 
processed the resulting files with optical character recognition (OCR) to facilitate search and data 
extraction. Searching for various keywords related to the equipment categories of interest allowed 
us to sort the files into folders by heat pump type, although we soon discovered that the paperwork 
associated with a given rebate number often included multiple types of equipment. The program 
implementor assigned a unique identifier to each customer’s rebate form, resulting in the creation 
of multiple IDs and redundant sets of documents for some projects.  The team extracted 
information for all relevant equipment types from these multi-measure invoices. 
We sampled invoices from the breadth of the furnished files for each heat pump type, while slightly 
favoring invoices that contained itemized cost information. 6  Where possible , we  recorded 
separate costs for the heat pump itself, installation labor, wiring, miscellaneous materials, duct 
work, controls, equipment removal, permits, and delivery. We also took care to exclude any non-
heat pump equipment such as furnaces, boilers, or humidifiers, when possible, or to discard the 
invoice when not possible . Similarly, we reversed any discounts or “prebates”7 applied by the 
contractor to reflect the true market rate cost of the work in our data. 
In addition to project cost details, NMR recorded the equipment model and any listed 
specifications, as well as the type of wiring work performed if applicable . We also transcribed 
project identifiers such as the rebate and application numbers, contractor details, installation date 
and address. NMR attempted to determine whether work was performed in an existing or newly 
constructed home  using keyword searches , 8  and recorded  this in the enhanced dataset  
(Section 1.3.5) with the rest of the details described here.

There are a limited number of websites that publish prices for the equipment categories examined 
in this study , making it difficult to mirror the (estimated) sourcing pattern of program partners. 
Moreover, although there are legitimate concerns about the use of a simple arithmetic average of 
published prices, weighting is not practical since the mix of represented channels will vary by 
model (e.g., due to manufacturer business relationships and other factors, scraped costs for a 
given model may not be available from multiple channels or even multiple sources). 
Although the analysis of web-scraped heat pump costs is restricted to complete compressor plus 
air handler packages, analogous to what would be installed in a home, the configurations of MSHP 
air handler head units can vary among retail packages. A 30 kBTUh MSHP package might include 
an 18 kBTUh ducted air handler and 12 kBTUh wall unit, or three 12 kBTUh wall units, etc. We 
would expect such disparate configurations to have different costs, even when they include the 
same model o f compressor , and similar issues affect other equipment types . We therefore 
developed a multi -tier approach to matching web-scraped with project  invoices, favoring more 
precise techniques over coarser methods where possible. The order of matching is as follows: 
1. Average cost for a listed AHRI number, since numbered ratings are given for specific 
compressor and air handler combinations. 
2. MSHP only: Average scraped cost by compressor model number and air handler 
configuration (i.e., the number of ducted units and the number of ductless units). 
3. Average scraped cost of all available packages for the same compressor model. 
4. Average cost for web -scraped equipment with the similar performance specifications 
determined by binning the scraped data (A.1.1). 
To provide a more precise cost comparison for MSHP, which account for two -thirds of the 2022 
heat pump project tracking data, we made use of data from a previous project. In addition to the 
web-scraping conducted expressly for this study, NMR previously collected MSHP costs for the 
MA 20R23 Residential New Construction Energy Optimization Cost Study in August 2020  from 
five of the same retailer and distributor sources used in October 2023.  With these older costs, 
which we paired with program projects in the same manner as those from 2023, it is possible to 
interpolate between them to the installation/invoice date of individual projects and better account 
for some changes in market conditions.

municipalities. Although the BLS published wages employed in this report’s analyses of regional

1.3.5 Enhanced Dataset 
The team prepared an enhanced dataset containing implementer program data, invoice details 
transcribed by NMR, equipment performance ratings, and web-scraped equipment costs of similar 
equipment. To preserve detail while minimizing complexity, the enhanced dataset includes 
multiple tables, with identifiers to link them together. 
Where possible the performance specifications —both heating and cooling capacities and 
efficiencies, particularly at 5 ° F —were determined by AHRI rating number. For MSHP, a 
secondary approach was to use the average specifications for a given heat pump model and air 
handler configuration. Finally, if other methods were not viable, we added the average of 
accessible ratings for a given heat pump model to the dataset.

1.3.7 Forecast 
Since chronological breadth of the data request materials is wider than anticipated, NMR sought 
to provide short-term forecasts of total project costs as an extension of the study’s goal to provide 
baseline metrics and also test out a model for the future iterations of this cost study. 
Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average  (ARIMA) and Seasonal Autoregressive Integrated 
Moving Average (SARIMA) are widely used time-series methods for forecasting. ARIMA relies on 
past values, including autoregressive and moving average components, to forecast future values. 
SARIMA, on the other hand, not only uses past values but also incorporates seasonality patterns. 
Because it includes  seasonality as an additional parameter, SARIMA is more effective than 
ARIMA in predicting complex data that exhibits cyclical trends. 
In our review of the normalized project cost data, we found a semi-annual cycle corresponding to 
the heating and cooling seasons with trailing shoulder months. As such, we determined that the 
best fit for quarterly invoice data was a SARIMA(p=0, d=0, q=0, P=0, D=1, Q=1, S=2) model. This 
incorporates a semi-annual cycle (S=2), a moving average between cycles (Q=1) and t he 
difference between the current cycle and its predecessor (D=1). Figure 4 shows an example of 
this lagged differencing. The original monthly average cost per ton (Jan. 2024$)  for MSHP is 
shown on the left, and processed data on the right. The “noisier” appearance of the latter is closer 
to the form of input preferred for forecasting.

Section 2 Ductless MSHP 
Ductless MSHP installations include single mini-split or multi -split heat pumps (MSHP) with a 
combination of wall-mount, ceiling cassette, or floor console air handlers. The installation costs of 
these projects are significantly different from those of ducted MSHP and mixed (ducted + ductless) 
projects, which are reported separately in Section 3. 
Unfortunately, we could not confidently classify the air handler configuration of most MassCEC 
projects and therefore omitted 1,887 of the 2,212 available invoices from this analysis. 
2.1 KEY FINDINGS 
➢ The installed cost for ductless MSHP projects h ave changed little  since 2019 , 
averaging $5,830 per ton of capacity across 2021 and 2022 ; adjusted to January 2024 
pricing (2.2). 
➢ However, equipment costs have increased significantly (2.2). 
➢ We detected no relationship between ductless MSHP project cost and the inclusion 
of other tasks such as the installation of a HPWH on an invoice (2.2.1). 
➢ There are regional differences in ductless MSHP project costs, primarily labor (2.7). 
2.2 CAPACITY-NORMALIZED PROJECT COST 
Figure 5 presents the average cost per ton of ductless MSHPs among invoices with no other work 
performed, or the sum of the following costs associated with ductless MSHP installations  from 
itemized invoices: heat pump equipment (including sales tax and delivery fees), miscellaneous 
materials, installation labor, wiring, controls, permits, and equipment removal. Although there is 
some volatility in the cost of  ductless MSHP, the long -term trend in inflation-adjusted dollars 
appears to be rather constant as indicated by the flat dotted trendline.13 In other words, capacity-
normalized total project costs appear to increase at nearly the same rate as general inflation.

becomes more prevalent, it also does not indicate that program incentives are appreciably driving 
costs up for this equipment category, at least for the time frame and sample analyzed in this

Section 3 Ducted and Mixed MSHP 
Ducted mini-split and multi-split (MSHP) installations include one or more air handlers connected 
with small duct systems. Since the installation costs of these projects are not significantly different 
from those of systems with a mix of ducted and ductless air handlers , we report on a combined 
sample of ducted and mixed projects. 
Unfortunately, we were not able to confidently classify the air handler configuration of most 
MassCEC projects and therefore omitted 1,887 of the 2,212 available invoices from this analysis. 
3.1 KEY FINDINGS 
➢ The installed cost for ducted/mixed MSHP projects sampled from the Mass Save 
program have decreased over time, averaging $6,914 per ton of capacity across 2021 
and 2022, adjusted to January 2024 pricing (3.2). 
➢ Like ductless MSHP , ducted/mixed MSHP equipment prices are increasing (3.4),  
but there is no significant trend in labor costs (3.5). 
➢ We detected no relationship between duct ed/mixed MSHP project cost and the 
inclusion of other tasks such as the installation of a HPWH on an invoice (3.2.1). 
➢ There are a few regional differences in ducted/mixed MSHP project costs, primarily 
due to labor (3.73.6).

Section 4 Centrally Ducted ASHP 
Centrally ducted air-source heat pumps (ASHP) are designed like central air conditioners (CAC). 
They feature a single compressor -coil pair connected to conventional ductwork and distribute 
conditioned air to an entire home  or large zone s within a home. This category of equipment is 
subject to the same federal regulations as MSHP but are typically less efficient than ducted and 
especially ductless MSHP , hence the differing program requirements in Table 2 and separate 
analysis here. 
For a comparison of ASHP and CAC, see Appendix B. 
4.1 KEY FINDINGS 
➢ The installed cost per ton of ASHP projects have remained consistent, averaging 
$6,560 per ton of capacity across 2021 and 2022 when adjusted to January 2024 dollars 
(4.2). 
➢ Yet the average proportion of project costs attributed to the ASHP itself has 
increased (4.4), while those of labor have decreased (4.5). 
➢ Multi-measure ASHP projects costs are 13% less per ton than ASHP-only projects 
(4.2.1), but there is no significant difference between MassCEC and Mass Save projects 
(4.2.2). 
4.2 CAPACITY-NORMALIZED PROJECT COST 
Figure 14  presents the average cost per ton of ASHP among invoices with no other work 
performed, or the sum of the following costs associated with ASHP installations from itemized 
invoices: heat pump equipment (including sales tax and delivery fees), miscellaneous materials, 
installation labor, wiring, ducts, controls, permits, and equipment removal. The long-term trend of 
ASHP project costs in inflation-adjusted dollars appears to be decreasing slightly, but this trend 
is not statistically significant . This does not support the premise that program incentives are 
driving costs up for this equipment category; instead, it conforms to the expectation that in a well-
functioning market, the cost of a technology in real dollars should decrease as it matures.

A.1 METHODOLOGY 
A.1.1 Web-Scraping 
Where model-specific methods were unable to yield a match, NMR used the average cost for 
web-scraped equipment with similar performance specifications. We first attempted to  match 
invoices to web-scraped heat pumps with identical efficiencies. Invoices that did not have an exact 
specification match were then paired with similarly rated web-scraped units, prioritizing the heat 
pump’s heating performance in the event of a tie or no match. For example, if there was no exact 
match for a 17.2 SEER, 8.1 HSPF unit, NMR tried to match to the average of all units within the 
17 SEER/8 HSPF bin. Failing that, the costs for other units with an 8.0–8.25 HSPF rating. 
The binning to determine similarity was performed by rounding efficiency ratings to the nearest 
multiple of the related unit granularity listed in Table 57. Note that HPWH matching employed tank 
volume rather than cooling efficiency as the second criteria, and that no specification binning was 
required; all HPWH were matched either by model, exact efficiency and volume, or exact 
efficiency.

Appendix B Central Air Conditioners 
Central air conditioners (CAC) are effectively heat pumps that only operate in cooling mode. 
B.1 KEY FINDINGS 
➢ As expected, duct and wiring proportions of installation costs for CAC and ASHP 
projects are similar, although the sample of projects with itemized costs is small. 
B.2 BACKGROUND 
NMR reviewed invoices for program -incentivized CAC installed when this equipment was still 
supported (pre-2022), and for non-incentivized systems uncovered during our examination of 
other rebate documentation. While we expected a small premium in ASHP equipment costs and 
total project costs relative to CAC,37 we also expected that other CAC costs would provide a useful 
comparison for ASHP given the similarity in design and installation processes i.e., installation 
labor, electrical work and ductwork costs should be comparable. 
Moreover, the program required cooling efficiency levels of 16 SEER or greater for both ASHP 
and CAC through 2021, increasing the similarity between the two samples for the most common 
efficiency rating of both equipment categories, as shown in Figure 26.38 The comparisons of CAC 
and ASHP costs that follow are for both 16 SEER -only invoices and the full sample of invoices. 
We have included the latter for two reasons: 1) 37% of the CAC invoices with itemized costs are 
not 16 SEER and 2) at least one -sixth of the CACs with efficiencies below 16 SEER were 
previously rated as 16 SEER by their manufacturers.39
