Executive Summary  
This memo presents the results of the Massachusetts heat pump incremental costs analysis. 
This study supports the Massachusetts Program Administrators (PAs) in their Three-Year 
Planning efforts to accurately estimate costs and benefits for heat pump fuel displacement 
program offerings, which incentivize the installation of air and ground source heat pumps 
(HPs) to displace pre-existing fuel fired (natural gas, oil, propane) or electric resistance 
heating equipment.   
This study leverages new HP cost research performed by NMR in 2023 and 2024 for the MA 
PAs. Recent NMR studies include an analysis of the MassCEC database and online 
webscraping data collection. These activities served to estimate recent HP installation costs 
and to understand the components of those costs, including equipment cost, installation, 
and labor costs.   
The incremental cost study also leveraged data from Guidehouse’s RES19 and RES23 
studies. Cost and efficiency data from the studies were compiled and used to inform 
equipment costs or to cross-validate costs from other studies. Costs from the prior studies 
were updated for inflation to 2023 dollars and adjusted based on incremental efficiency 
differences between the prior study source and the incremental cost study equipment 
efficiencies for program rebated heat pumps, new equipment, and existing equipment. 
Table ES-1 through Table ES-3 summarizes the key findings of this research for each of 
three baseline scenarios. The three baseline scenarios are as follows and are described in 
detail in Section 1.5. 
• Market Rate Program Data Designation Scenario: This scenario summarizes costs for 
market rate heat pump program participants. This scenario includes the baseline 
equipment weights for all heating and cooling equipment types. Heat pump usage for the 
Full Displacement measure category is based on the full Heat Pump Metering Study 
metered sample, where the displacement categorizes are defined in the program 
tracking database.  
• Market Rate Alternative Scenario: This scenario summarizes costs for market rate 
heat pump program participants. This scenario removes the baseline weights for 
baseline heat pump types and re-weights the remaining equipment types to 100%. Heat 
pump usage for the Full Displacement measure category is based on the subset of the

Heat Pump Metering Study metered sample where the customer either removed or 
disconnected their pre-existing heating equipment.  
• Income Eligible Scenario: This scenario summarizes costs for income eligible heat 
pump program participants. The baseline equipment type is assumed to be the pre-
existing system types per the measure name. Because this Incremental Cost study 
assumes zero cost for pre-existing equipment, this scenario results in an incremental 
cost identical to the new equipment cost. 
For the market rate scenarios, the baseline equipment costs represent the blended average 
of costs for the equipment the customer would have installed if they did not install the HP 
they did, which could have been keeping their pre-existing HVAC system installed (assumed 
to be zero cost) or installing an alternative piece of equipment (noted as “New" equipment in 
this study). The HP installation costs are broken out for a new unit (at a base efficiency 
level), and for a higher efficiency HP unit representing a program-average installation. 
Results are provided for three incremental cost scenarios: 
• Baseline equipment to new unit heat pump 
• New unit heat pump to program rebated heat pump 
• Baseline equipment to program rebated heat pump 
These costs are summarized for each of the program measures, which vary based on 
installed HP type, displacement scenario (full or partial displacement), and the customer’s 
pre-existing heating fuel type. The costs are normalized by the rated cooling tonnage of the 
installed HP equipment.

1. Methodology 
Guidehouse estimated heat pump equipment and installation costs using the results of a 
2023 NMR draft memo1 which evaluated invoices from the Massachusetts Clean Energy 
Center (MassCEC) public database, along with results from a subsequent NMR 
Webscraping effort. 
Guidehouse estimated baseline equipment costs using studies developed in 2018, including 
the MA Water Heating, Boiler, and Furnace Cost Study (RES19)2, and MA Cost Study of 
Heat Pump Installations for Dual Fuel Operation (RES23)3. 
Once primary data sources were identified for each piece of equipment, Guidehouse used 
inflation and price index values to update all costs to 2023 dollars. The team also estimated 
costs at varying efficiency levels, leveraging regression analysis performed in the various 
studies to correlate installed equipment prices with various equipment characteristics, such 
as size and efficiency.  
For the market rate scenarios, Guidehouse applied a series of baseline equipment cost 
weights using the counterfactual condition: what equipment the customer would have 
installed if they did not install the heat pump they did, which could have been install no unit 
(for cooling), keep the existing cooling or heating units installed, installing an alternative 
piece of equipment (assumed to be a new unit efficiency), or indicating that the installed 
heat pump was the only unit type considered. For the income eligible scenario, the baseline 
equipment type is assumed to be the pre-existing equipment type(s).  
Incremental cost was defined as the difference between a baseline equipment cost and a 
more-efficient equipment cost, inclusive of equipment labor, materials, taxes, and other 
install-related costs. Incremental cost was calculated for several points, including the 
blended baseline cost to the program-rebated efficient heat pump, blended baseline costs to 
new unit heat pump, and new unit heat pump to program-rebated heat pump.  
1.1 Heat Pump Cost Assumptions 
The following section provides a detailed overview of data sources used and the underlying 
analysis methodology within each source. 
NMR MassCEC Heat Pump Invoice Analysis 
In November 2023, NMR shared a draft memo with Guidehouse which included heat pump 
project cost results as sourced from the MassCEC database.4 The review was conducted for 
Ground Source Heat Pumps (GSHP), Central Heat Pumps (CHP), and Mini-Split Heat 
Pumps (MSHP), and focused on publicly available MassCEC datasets. The study pulled 
data from residential-scale projects within the commercial ASHP program and records from 
the residential ASHP and whole home pilot programs. The final sample included 206 GSHP

collected rated efficiency values and unit capacities. Guidehouse used this data for 
developing and validating equipment efficiency cost regressions. 
1.2 Baseline Equipment Cost Assumptions 
The following studies were used to inform the baseline equipment costs. The costs from 
each study were adjusted for 2023 dollars using Consumer Price Index (CPI) data.5 
MA Water Heating, Boiler, and Furnace Cost Study – RES19 (2018) 
This study evaluated the costs for single family installations of water heaters, boilers, and 
furnaces rebated through the Massachusetts Residential Heating and Cooling program. It 
assessed consumer costs for purchase and installation of these HVAC equipment types and 
analyzed the relationship between cost and equipment efficiency.  
Guidehouse used the RES19 study’s average itemized costs by category for each 
equipment type and efficiency level to develop cost estimates for baseline gas-fired 
equipment.  
MA Cost Study of Heat Pump Installations for Dual Fuel Operation – RES23 (2018) 
This study provides itemized install costs for residential central A/C and heat pump products 
installed in Massachusetts. The authors analyzed 7,948 Mass Save rebate records 
submitted between November 2016 and July 2017, and used a set of 200 total invoice 
records to supplement the analysis.  
Guidehouse used the RES23 study to source cost information for existing central AC 
equipment.

1.3 Baseline and Program Rebated Equipment Efficiency 
Assumptions 
Table 1 shows efficiency assumptions for existing, new, and program rebated (EE) HVAC 
equipment. These values are used in the incremental cost analysis to scale the equipment 
costs based on the rated efficiency of the equipment. Several of the cost data sources 
include regressions to adjust equipment costs based on efficiency, or lookup tables for 
equipment costs based on different efficiency levels.  
The following data sources and assumptions were used to inform these baseline and post-
retrofit equipment efficiency assumptions:  
• Existing unit efficiency values are sourced from the most recent Massachusetts 
Residential Building Use and Equipment Characterization Study (RBUECS), updated in 
2023.6 The existing unit efficiencies are the median unit efficiencies for all HVAC units 
installed in the 2023 onsite metering sample (n=269 sites in onsite sample). The existing 
unit efficiency values are used for customers who indicate that they would have left their 
existing HVAC system in place if they did not install the heat pump they did.         
• New unit efficiency values are sourced from the MA RBUECS survey data collection 
and subsequent characterization analysis and are the median efficiency values for all 
units installed from 2020-2023. These efficiency values are used for customers who 
indicate that they would have installed an alternative HVAC system if they did not install 
the heat pump they did. Based on discussions with the MA PAs and EEAC consultants, 
the team assumes new unit efficiencies of 15 SEER and 8.8 HSPF for central heat pump 
based on current code minimum values, and 17 SEER and 9.5 HSPF for mini-split heat 
pumps to estimate typical standard efficiencies for mini-split heat pumps available on the 
market.  
• Program rebated (EE) unit efficiency values are sourced from 2022 Mass Save Fuel 
Displacement program tracking dataset, delivered by DNV. The program average 
efficiency values represent the median unit efficiency values for all units installed in 
2022.

1.4 Equipment Cost Normalization and Aggregation 
Guidehouse used the NMR MassCEC memo to source post-retrofit equipment costs and 
used the RES19 and RES23 studies to source baseline equipment costs, and then made 
updates based on unit efficiency levels. The cost adjustment methodology is shown below: 
Incremental Efficiency Cost Factor 
The team applied an adjustment factor to scale the efficiency and related equipment costs 
between the source dataset and the efficiency value assumptions used for this analysis. The 
NMR webscraping data was used to develop adjustment factors for baseline existing and 
new units as well as the program rebated heat pump equipment. The RES19 study was 
used to develop adjustment factors for baseline existing and new unit fossil fuel equipment. 
Incremental efficiency cost factor between two pieces of equipment (unit “a” and unit “b”), 
expressed in dollars per ton per unit efficiency, is defined in Equation 1:

(𝐶𝑜𝑜𝑙𝐸𝑓𝑓𝑎 −  𝐶𝑜𝑜𝑙𝐸𝑓𝑓𝑏)/3.412 + (𝐻𝑒𝑎𝑡𝐸𝑓𝑓𝑎 −  𝐻𝑒𝑎𝑡𝐸𝑓𝑓𝑏)

1.6 Incremental Cost Comparison 
Guidehouse compared incremental costs for “Blended Baseline to Heat Pump – Program 
Rebated” against the MA 2022-2024 Plan BCR Model measure inputs.7 Table 10 shows the 
results of this comparison, using the Market Rate Program Data Designation Scenario. 
GSHP measure incremental cost is significantly higher in comparison to the 2022-2024 BCR 
model, likely due to the prior assumption not accounting for the full installation costs of 
GSHPs. Most of the remaining measures have similar incremental costs compared to the 
previous BCR assumption, likely because the previous BCR Model includes a deferred 
replacement credit which accounts for existing equipment being replaced before end of 
useful life.
